Plumbing in a Slab Foundation: A Fernley, NV Homeowner Guide

When pipes sit in or under concrete, repairs work differently. Here is what lives in the slab, what fails and what your options are.

By Fernley 24/7 Plumbing · Published November 16, 2026 · Updated November 16, 2026

In a slab foundation home, the drain pipes are usually laid in the ground or in a gravel bed before the concrete is poured. The slab then sits on top. Supply lines may run through the attic or walls, or in some homes under the slab. Because drain lines are buried, a blockage or a break is harder to reach than in a house with a crawlspace.

For most owners, the practical result is simple. Know where your cleanouts are, watch for warning signs and call early. A small problem that is easy to fix at the cleanout can turn into a slab repair if left alone.

Layout

What runs in, under and around a slab

Knowing the layout helps you spot problems early.

Drain, waste and vent lines

Drain lines slope toward the sewer, and are buried below the slab. They carry waste from toilets, tubs and sinks. Vents rise through walls to the roof.

Water supply lines

Cold and hot lines may run through attics, walls or, in some homes, under the slab. Where they run depends on when and how the house was built.

Cleanouts and shutoffs

A cleanout is a capped access point for drain lines. The main water shutoff is usually near the meter or where the line enters the house.

Warning signs

Warning signs of a slab plumbing problem

Concrete hides problems. These signs do not.

Warm or damp floor spots

A leaking hot line under concrete can warm the floor. A damp spot on tile or carpet can mark a leak.

Unexplained high water bill

If the meter moves with everything off, a leak may be running. See leak detection.

Slow drains in several fixtures

A buried drain line that is partly blocked or sagging drains slowly throughout.

Cracks or sticking doors

New cracks in walls or floors and doors that stick can follow soil changes under the slab.

Repairs

Why slab foundations change how a plumber works

On a crawlspace home, a plumber can reach pipes from below. On a slab, access is through walls, ceilings, cleanouts or the slab itself. That affects the choice of repair. A reroute through the attic avoids breaking concrete. A spot repair opens a small area of the slab. A repipe replaces failing lines in walls and ceilings.

We use acoustic and thermal tools to find leaks before opening concrete, so the repair area is as small as possible. We explain the options and the price before work starts.

Know your cleanout before you need it

A cleanout lets a plumber clear a drain line without opening walls or the slab. Find yours now, usually a capped pipe near the foundation or in the yard, and keep it uncovered.

If you cannot find it, we can locate it during a service visit. See drain cleaning for what happens when a drain backs up, and mark the cleanout so it is easy to find next time.

Your checklist

Planning ahead: what homeowners can do now

If you own a slab home, spend an hour learning the basics. Find the main water shutoff and test it. Find the drain cleanout and keep it uncovered. Take a photo of the water meter with every fixture off so you have a baseline for later comparisons.

Keep a folder with the permits, plans and repair invoices you have. A plumber who sees where past work was done can plan a repair faster, and an insurer or a buyer later will want the record. This is a small habit that pays off the day something goes wrong.

Remodeling

Renovations that touch the slab

Slab homes can be remodeled, but each plan needs its own check.

Moving a bathroom

New drain lines may need trenches cut through the slab. That adds cost and permit steps.

Adding a kitchen island sink

A drain must reach the main line with proper slope and a vent, often through a cut slab.

Converting a garage

Floor drains and a new water heater location both need planning before drywall.

Upgrading supply lines

A reroute through walls or attic can avoid slab work. See repiping.

Ground water and slab homes in Fernley, Nevada

Fernley's history includes a January 5, 2008 breach of the Truckee Canal levee that flooded roughly 580 homes on the east side and forced about 3,500 residents to evacuate. New homes there were later built above street level. The event is a reminder that water moves through this ground.

If a slab edge stays damp or a yard near the canal stays soggy, mention it to the plumber, since a pipe leak and ground water can look alike. The City of Fernley Building Department at 775-784-9900 can explain permit needs for slab-related repairs.
